In the summer of 2024, as a participant in the University of Kansas Research Experience for Undergraduates (REU), I did research on osmolyte interactions with peptides. This project involved running many simulations on the university HPC cluster, writing custom Python code to gather thermodynamic information from simulation trajectories, and using statistical mechanics to answer questions that experimental techniques are insufficient to answer. In this project, I learned how to use OpenMM and LAMMPS for molecular dynamics simulations and Julia and NumPy for scientific trajectory analysis.

Osmolytes are small organic molecules that accumulate in cells in response to osmotic stress. These molecules can stabilize or destabilize the folded state of proteins, but the mechanism through which that happens is not precisely understood. One theory is that osmolytes directly interact with the protein chain, and that in that interaction they change the favorability of certain protein configurations. Another theory is that osmolytes instead change the water structure around the protein, and that change in the water structure is what affects the protein configuration. Because proteins serve a wide range of biological functions depending on their folded configuration, distinguishing between these effects could help probe certain health conditions, advance drug development, and improve understanding of biological signaling mechanisms.

I specifically investigated the effects of two osmolytes, trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) and urea, on the leucine dipeptide. We chose TMAO and urea because previous group members had already studied those osmolytes' effects on water, so we already knew how to simulate those osmolytes in molecular dynamics simulations. Secondly, we chose to use a dipeptide instead of a full protein to enable us to run longer simulations with shorter timesteps, increasing the accuracy of our thermodynamic calculations.

To study the effect of TMAO and urea on water–dipeptide systems, we used classical molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Classical MD simulations treat each atom in the system as a point particle whose forces are determined by potential energy fields defined by the positions of all the atoms. Newton's equations of motion are then integrated numerically to give the position of each atom at each timestep in the trajectory. These simulations were performed using LAMMPS at first and then OpenMM on the University of Kansas HPC cluster. After the simulations were completed, I wrote code to analyze the trajectories in both Python with NumPy and Julia. The simulations were run under two different conditions, NVT and NVE. In the former, the number of atoms, the volume of the box, and the temperature are held constant, and in the latter, the energy is held constant instead of the temperature.

Many chemical properties of interest can be expressed as the temperature derivative of some quantity. For example, the activation energy of diffusion, which represents how much energy a given water molecule must have in order to diffuse through the system, can be expressed as the derivative of the diffusion coefficient with respect to the temperature of the system. Although this quantity is expressed in terms of a temperature derivative, it says something about the water structure and mechanism at a given temperature.

In chemistry, these derivatives are typically measured by making measurements of the quantity of interest (in this case, the diffusion coefficient) at different temperatures and then finding the temperature derivative analytically. In biological systems, this method is ineffective because proteins unfold and fold as temperature changes, so there is not a linear relationship between the quantity of interest and the temperature. Nevertheless, the temperature derivative is still useful because it provides information about the structure and mechanisms of the system at a given temperature. To calculate this derivative, we use fluctuation theory, which lets you extract temperature-derivative quantities from simulations at a single temperature.

We found that urea molecules sit closer to the dipeptide than TMAO molecules do. This suggests that urea binds directly to proteins, while TMAO has an indirect effect through the water structure. This is shown in Figure 2, where the osmolyte–dipeptide distance is plotted for both TMAO and urea.

Secondly, our findings suggest a temperature dependence of the concentration effect on the placement of urea molecules. This is shown in the entropic and energetic breakdown of Figure 3. Greater concentration of urea decreases the barrier for urea molecules to leave the dipeptide's first solvation shell, but this is counteracted by entropic effects, so as the entropic contribution becomes larger at higher temperatures, we expect these effects to become unbalanced.

Finally, both TMAO and urea reduce the diffusion of water, but the process is energetically driven in TMAO and entropically driven in urea. Figures 4 and 5 show that the diffusion of water in TMAO and urea slows at higher concentrations of the osmolytes. However, from the activation energy, we can see that the TMAO process is slower because of a higher activation energy, while the water diffusion in urea is slower despite a constant activation energy.

Overall, my work at the University of Kansas REU helps advance understanding of the mechanism by which osmolytes affect the protein folding and unfolding process. The fact that urea has a direct effect on proteins, and that this effect is mediated by temperature, suggests the ability to use osmolytes like urea in important biological reactions. Furthermore, understanding TMAO's effect on the water structure can shed light on solvent effects in pharmaceuticals. If I continued this project, I would run simulations with other dipeptides and osmolytes to determine how stable these results are. I would also use other thermodynamic techniques, such as measuring hydrogen bond exchange, to probe exactly the effect urea has on proteins.
